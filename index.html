Spotting unusual moves in ACB’s stock price

Can one model forecast next week’s return and flag when a week is abnormal, without peeking at the future?

0.94recall at 1.0σ (best baseline 0.32)
3anomaly thresholds tested: 0.5σ, 1.0σ, 1.5σ
80/20time-ordered train/test split

In plain words

The question

Can we get an early warning when a stock makes an unusually large weekly move? Banks and risk teams care, because sudden swings signal risk or opportunity.

What I did

I took about 7.5 years of ACB Bank’s daily prices (383 weeks, 2017 to March 2025) and defined an “unusual” week at three levels of strictness. I trained a neural network, FineNet, to predict next week’s return and say whether the week will be unusual, then tested it on the latest 20% of the timeline against three simpler methods.

What I found

At the standard threshold it caught 94% of the unusual weeks, versus 32% for the best simpler method. It is not more precise: about half of its alerts were real (0.50, against 0.56 for GRU and DRNN). So it rarely misses a big move, at the price of some false alarms. About 25% of all weeks count as unusual at 1.0σ, and weekly returns average +0.62% with a typical swing of 5.85%.

Why it matters

For a risk team, missing a big move usually costs more than a false alarm, so recall is the number to optimize. A person can filter false alarms; nobody can act on a warning that never came.

Limits and next steps

This is one stock, while the original paper used about 300 companies, so I read the pattern rather than the exact numbers. Next: test more tickers and judge alerts by their cost, not only precision and recall.

How the model is built

Price window→Dilated CNN→BiGRU→Weekly ROI+Abnormal flag

The dilated CNN reads patterns across short and long stretches of the series, and the BiGRU reads the sequence forwards and backwards. One network, two outputs: this is FineNet (Tsai et al., RecSys ’19), which I reimplemented.

Step by step

  1. CollectACB Bank daily prices from 2017 onward.
  2. Define “abnormal”Computed weekly ROI and labelled a week abnormal when it sat beyond k standard deviations, for k = 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5.
  3. Prepare inputsClosing price and weekly ROI are MinMax-scaled, then cut into sliding windows of 10 steps.
  4. Split honestlyTrain on the earlier 80% of the timeline and test on the latest 20%, so nothing from the future leaks into training.
  5. Train and compareFineNet (parallel Conv1D branches, some dilated, into a BiGRU, with one head regressing the next value and one classifying anomalies) against ARIMA, a plain GRU and a stacked-LSTM DRNN. ARIMA is evaluated walk-forward on real past prices.

Result

0.94FineNet
0.32Best baseline

Recall is the share of real anomalies the model catches, which is why it is the headline number here: a missed unusual move is the costly mistake.

Is an income model fair to everyone it scores?

Predicting income is the easy part. I wanted to know who the model gets wrong more often, and what it costs to fix that.

86.69%accuracy, AUC 0.924
0.154 → 0.004sex Demographic Parity gap
2.2 ptsaccuracy given up for fairness

In plain words

The question

If a lender or employer used a model to predict who earns more than $50K, would it treat men and women, and different racial groups, equally?

What I did

I used 30,162 US census records to build an accurate income model (86.7% correct), then checked it group by group: how often does it say “high income”, and how often is it wrong, for each sex, race and their combinations? I applied a correction technique (ExponentiatedGradient) to even out outcomes between men and women, and re-checked everything.

What I found

Before the fix, the model labelled women high-income far less often (a gap of 0.154 in selection rates). After the fix the gap fell to 0.004, for a 2.2-point drop in accuracy. But fixing one measure made another worse: the gap in error rates between the sexes rose from 0.048 to 0.297, and the race and sex-by-race gaps barely moved. SHAP shows marital status and the “husband” category among the strongest signals, and both are tied to sex, which is how bias can leak in.

Why it matters

Accuracy alone hides unfairness. A responsible team has to choose which fairness definition fits the use case, accept the trade-off and document it.

Limits and next steps

Only sex was constrained, and the data is a historic 1994 census sample, so this demonstrates a method rather than a deployable lending model. Next: constrain several attributes together and compare mitigation methods.

The two fairness checks, in plain words

Demographic Parity: every group should receive positive predictions at a similar rate.
Equalized Odds: every group should see similar error rates.

Step by step

  1. DataAbout 32.5K census rows from the UCI Adult Income dataset. Rows with missing values (about 7.4%) were dropped, leaving 30,162.
  2. ModelA Random Forest inside a leakage-safe pipeline (encoders fit on the training set only), tuned with RandomizedSearchCV: 3-fold stratified CV, optimizing AUC.
  3. AuditFairlearn measured the gaps by sex, race and the two combined. SHAP showed which features drive the predictions.
  4. MitigateExponentiatedGradient applied to the sex gap.
  5. Re-checkThe sex parity gap fell sharply. Equalized Odds for sex got worse, and the race and intersectional gaps barely moved. I reported all of it.

Result

0.154Parity gap before
0.004Parity gap after
86.69%Accuracy before
84.52%Accuracy after (2.2 points lower)

What stood out: optimizing one fairness definition did not improve the other. Equalized Odds for sex rose from 0.048 to 0.297, and the sex × race Equalized Odds gap stayed at 1.0. Fixing one attribute at a time does not guarantee fairness at the intersection.

Sorting 50,000 Walmart customers into four groups

Which kinds of shoppers are in the data, and what should the business do differently for each?

50,000customer records, 12 columns
k = 4chosen with elbow and silhouette (about 0.35 at k = 4)
Ledteam of 3. I did the outline, introduction, data cleaning, preparation and model building

Course project for Introduction to Data Science (INS3254.05), International School, VNU. Data: Walmart Customer Purchase Behavior on Kaggle.

In plain words

The question

Walmart serves millions of shoppers. Who are they in practice, and how should marketing treat different customers?

What I did

I took 50,000 purchase records (age, amount, rating, category, payment method, discount use, repeat purchase), confirmed there were no missing or duplicate rows, explored the data, and grouped customers by behavior with K-Means. Two statistical checks (elbow and silhouette) pointed to four groups, which I profiled and drew on a 2D map.

What I found

The four groups are almost equal in size (about 12,300 to 12,650 customers each). Spending (about 255 per purchase), age (about 39) and satisfaction (about 3 out of 5) are the same in every group. What separates customers is behavior: do they use discounts, and do they come back? That gives four types: loyal discount users, loyal full-price buyers, one-time discount buyers and one-time full-price buyers.

Why it matters

Marketing can be targeted: reward loyalty where customers already return, and use vouchers or quality guarantees to win a second purchase from one-time buyers.

Limits and next steps

The data is unusually uniform (purchase amounts are evenly spread and splits are close to 50/50), so I would validate the segments on real transaction data and measure outcomes such as repeat rate after each campaign.

Step by step

  1. Read the data50,000 rows and 12 columns: age, gender, city, category, product, purchase date and amount, payment method, discount applied, rating, repeat customer.
  2. Check qualityNo missing values and no duplicate rows. The City column was dropped.
  3. ExplorePurchase amounts are spread evenly from 0 to 500. Spending and ratings look the same across categories, discounts and repeat customers split roughly 50/50, and each month has about 4,000 transactions. So behavior, not category or season, is what could separate customers.
  4. PrepareYes/No columns encoded as 1/0, Category and Payment Method one-hot encoded, and Age, Purchase Amount, Discount Applied and Repeat Customer standardized with StandardScaler.
  5. Choose kK-Means for k = 2 to 10 (random_state 42, n_init 10). WCSS drops sharply up to k = 4 and flattens after, and the silhouette score peaks at k = 4.
  6. Train and profileFit K-Means with k = 4, then summarize each cluster: size, spending, discount rate, repeat rate, rating, age, top category and top payment method.
  7. Visualize and recommendPCA to two dimensions, box plots, bar charts, and an action for each segment.
